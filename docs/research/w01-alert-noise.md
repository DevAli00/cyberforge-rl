# W1 research: how noisy are real SOC alerts?

Status: research note, 2026-10-18. It changes nothing by itself. Any change to the game goes through a spec PR that Ali reviews.

Scope: false-alarm share, alert volume vs. analyst capacity, the noise mix by alert type, severities, and which machines false alarms land on. Attack progression (stages, timing, targeting) is covered in a separate note: `docs/research/w01-attack-progression.md` on branch `w01/research-attack-progression`.

Source types used below:

- **telemetry**: counted from real alerts.
- **survey**: people asked for their opinion or estimate.
- **rule pack**: severity a vendor or community ships by default (Sigma, Elastic, Microsoft).
- **vendor marketing**: vendor numbers with no method given (avoided where possible).

## Summary

1. **Our ~90% noise is in the right range.** Measured data puts false or harmless alerts at 81–86% of graded incidents (Microsoft, telemetry) and just under 90% of alerts analysts checked (Kaspersky MDR, telemetry). Counted per raw alert it is far higher: 0.01% of alerts tied to real attacks (USENIX 2024, telemetry).
2. **No public source gives the mix of false-alarm types.** No report says what share of false alarms are phishing, USB, AV and so on. All 12 `noise_freq` values stay **our assumption**. I propose no new list (see §4). The best next step is to compute the mix ourselves from Microsoft's public GUIDE dataset.
3. **Five severities disagree with the default rule packs.** The biggest gap: `internal_port_scan` is rated *low* by both Sigma and Elastic. We have 4. Phishing click, cloud key and impossible travel are rated higher than ours. AV hits are rated *high*, not *critical*.
4. **Scanning is the loudest thing on a network sensor.** In a real SOC, 96.7% of blocked attack attempts were network scans. Legitimate internal scanners alone raised ~195k harmless alerts. This supports "port scans are noisy". It also strains our design rule, because scanning is a stage-3 alert type (see §2).
5. **Volume vs. capacity doesn't match any single source.** Surveys say analysts handle about 1 alert in 3 and ~10–20 alerts per day each. Our game: 1 in 10, and with the proposed 12-hour turn only ~2 per day. There is no public data on which machines false alarms land on.

## 2. Notes per finding

**Overall noise ([S1]–[S5]).** Every source agrees most alerts are not real attacks. How much depends on what is counted. Per *graded incident*: 81–86% (Microsoft). Per *alert an analyst looked at*: ~90% (Kaspersky). Per *raw network alert*: 99.99% (USENIX 2024). Our game counts alerts that reach the analyst's inbox, so the 80–90% band fits best. The USENIX paper adds a nuance: much "noise" is real but harmless. 49% of alerts were *benign triggers* (rule fired correctly, activity was allowed, e.g. a vulnerability scanner). 27% were *failed attack attempts*. Our game lumps both into "false alarm", which is fine for W1.

**Severity ([S6], [S9], [S10]).** Most of our severities are within one step of the default rule packs. Five are off by one step or more (table above). Port scan is the clear miss: both Sigma and Elastic ship scan detections at *low*. Changing severities only affects the severity-first baseline player, not the game's reward.

**Scanning and the design rule ([S3]).** At the SOC in [S3], scanning dominated the alerts: 96.7% of blocked attempts. Legitimate internal scans were a major harmless trigger. Matching this would make `internal_port_scan` even noisier. That clashes with "late ones stay reliable" for a stage-3 type. Flag only; this is Ali's call.

**Mix of false-alarm types.** I found no public breakdown of false alarms by alert type. Sigma's `falsepositives` field gives a hint about which rules are noisy: *likely* for USB, *legitimate admin activity* for new admin / user creation, *VPN* for impossible travel, *unlikely* for AV and ransomware signatures. But it gives no rates.

## 3. Volume vs. capacity, and the turn length

The attack-progression note proposes **1 turn ≈ 12 hours**. Under that, one analyst sees ~18 false alarms per day and investigates 2. The sources say:

| Quantity | Game (12 h turn) | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alerts investigated per analyst per day | 2 | 65% of analysts handle under 20; 30% under 10 | [S7] survey |
| Share of incoming alerts handled | 10% | ~33% (67% not dealt with) | [S4] survey |
| Alerts arriving per SOC per day | 18 (one analyst) | 4,484 (whole SOC) | [S4] survey |

So with 12 h turns, the analyst is ~5–10× slower than surveyed analysts. To match "~10–20 investigations per day", a turn would be ~1 hour. The attack note says that would need `p_advance` ≈ 0.13–0.18. I don't propose a change here. Turn length is a joint decision with the attack-progression research. Note also that "handle 1 in 3" (Vectra) and "~90% noise" can't both hold in our game while all alerts are visible. Fewer arrivals per turn would push the noise share down to ~70%.

## 4. noise_freq: no new list proposed

No public source gives per-type false-alarm shares, so I don't propose new `noise_freq` values. Inventing a list would break the "match the data" rule. The current values (sum = 1.00) stay labelled "our assumption".

**How to get real numbers:** Microsoft's GUIDE dataset ([S1]) is public (Kaggle, CDLA-2.0 licence). It has per-alert `Category` (MITRE tactic), `DetectorId` and an analyst `IncidentGrade` (true positive / benign positive / false positive). Grouping grades by category would give measured "how often is this kind of alert harmless" numbers. It's not per rule name, but close enough to map most of our 12 types. It needs a Kaggle login and a multi-GB download, so it's out of scope for this note.
